# Catholic Timeline — Image Generation Guidelines

## Purpose

Create historically believable, photorealistic images that give the
viewer a window into the lives of saints, biblical figures, and
important events in Catholic history\.

The goal is not to create conventional devotional portraits\. The goal is
to make the viewer feel as though a photographer traveled back in time
and captured a real moment\.

## 1\. Read the Article First

Before creating an image prompt, read the complete article\.

Determine: \- Who is involved \- When and where the event occurred \- The
person’s age at that point in life \- What the person was doing \- Their
vocation, occupation, or role \- Important people who may have been
present \- Clothing appropriate to the period and location \- Architecture
and environment \- Geographic setting \- Significant objects mentioned in
the story \- Emotional character of the moment

The image should illustrate the story, not merely the person’s
appearance\.

## 2\. Prefer a Moment, Not a Portrait

The default image should be a candid moment from the person’s life\.

Avoid having the subject: \- Stand and pose for the viewer \- Look
directly at the camera \- Stand motionless in the center of the frame \-
Hold symbolic objects simply to identify themselves \- Appear like a
formal saint portrait

Instead, show the person doing something meaningful: writing, teaching,
traveling, preaching, working, praying, serving, caring for someone,
conversing, studying, or participating in the historical event\.

The viewer should feel that the person has been caught naturally in the
middle of life\.

## 3\. Choose the Most Meaningful Scene

Select a scene that communicates something important about who this
person was\.

When the article contains a major historical event, favor that event:
conversion, apparition, council, missionary encounter, founding,
important teaching, service to the poor, imprisonment, martyrdom,
significant meeting, scholarly work, Eucharistic event, or meaningful
prayer\.

Do not automatically choose the most dramatic scene\. Choose the scene
that best communicates the person’s life, vocation, holiness, or
historical importance\.

## 4\. Historical Accuracy

Treat every image as a historical reconstruction\.

Pay careful attention to: \- Exact century or year \- Country and region \-
Ethnicity and physical appearance \- Approximate age \- Hairstyles and
facial hair \- Clothing and religious habits \- Clerical vestments \-
Tools, furniture, books, and manuscripts \- Transportation \-
Architecture \- Streets and churches \- Landscape and vegetation \- Weather
and climate

Avoid anachronisms\. When depicting a known historical location,
reconstruct what it looked like at the time of the event, not what
exists there today\.

## 5\. Use the Article as the Primary Source

Do not automatically perform external research for every image\.

Classify the article:

### A — Sufficient

The article provides enough information to create a historically
plausible image\. Proceed directly\.

### B — Minor Gaps

Small visual details are missing but can reasonably be inferred from the
time and place\. Proceed without additional research\.

### C — Research Required

Research only when an important visual fact cannot safely be determined
from the article\.

Examples include the historical appearance of a location, exact
religious habit, known physical appearance, unusual vestments,
architecture at a particular date, people present at an apparition, or
geography surrounding an event\.

Research should answer the specific visual question rather than
researching the entire biography again\.

## 6\. Photorealism

Images should look like high\-quality historical photography\.

Prefer natural skin, realistic eyes and hands, natural expressions, fine
clothing texture, realistic materials, natural depth of field,
physically plausible lighting, subtle imperfections, and believable
human interaction\.

Avoid painting\-like appearance, fantasy art, plastic skin, excessive
HDR, heavy cinematic grading, or artificially perfect faces\.

The ideal impression is: **“This looks like a photograph taken at the**
**actual historical event\.”**

## 7\. Lighting

Historical does not mean dark\.

Images should generally be bright, clear, naturally illuminated, and
easy to view on a phone screen\.

Avoid: \- Dark brown rooms \- Brick\-red color casts \- Excessive amber or
orange \- Sepia coloration \- Heavy shadows or crushed blacks \- Heavy
chiaroscuro \- Dim faces \- Small pools of candlelight surrounded by
darkness \- Artificial cinematic gloom

For daytime interiors, favor abundant natural daylight from historically
plausible windows, doors, courtyards, cloisters, and openings\.

Faces, hands, clothing, and important environmental details should be
clearly visible\. Prefer neutral daylight and realistic colors\. White
clothing should look approximately white rather than beige or orange\.

Candles, oil lamps, fireplaces, and torches may appear when appropriate,
but should not automatically determine the entire color palette\.

## 8\. Favor Naturally Bright Locations

Whenever historically plausible, consider outdoor landscapes, gardens,
cloisters, courtyards, streets, workshops, schools, bright churches,
homes with daylight, fields, roads, villages, and public squares\.

Do not repeatedly place historical figures alone in small, dark rooms\.

## 9\. Color

Use realistic natural colors appropriate to the place and period\.

Do not apply a universal “old world” filter\. Avoid making every
historical scene predominantly brown, beige, amber, orange, brick red,
or gray\.

History itself was not sepia\-colored\.

## 10\. Composition

Default image format: **portrait 2:3 / 4×6 composition**\.

Compose for a phone display: \- Subject clearly identifiable \- Enough
background to establish place and time \- Natural positioning \- People
interacting naturally \- Subject slightly off\-center when appropriate \-
Visual depth using foreground, subject, and background \- Avoid excessive
empty space \- Do not crop important faces, hands, religious objects,
tools, or actions

## 11\. Human Interaction

People should behave naturally\.

When multiple people are present, they should look at and respond to one
another when appropriate\. Hands should perform believable actions\.
People should not all face the viewer or appear arranged for a group
photograph\.

The scene should feel observed rather than staged\.

## 12\. Expressions

Avoid exaggerated religious expressions\.

Do not automatically depict saints with eyes dramatically raised toward
heaven, folded hands, artificial smiles, or constant ecstatic
expressions\.

Use expressions appropriate to the activity: concentration, compassion,
joy, fatigue, concern, prayer, determination, curiosity, conversation,
or contemplation\.

Holiness should primarily be communicated through the person’s actions
and story rather than theatrical facial expressions\.

## 13\. Halos and Supernatural Effects

Do not automatically add halos, glowing circles, gold disks, artificial
rays, mystical fog, sparkles, fantasy lighting, or unexplained
supernatural illumination\.

When supernatural phenomena are explicitly part of the event, depict
them carefully according to the historical account\.

## 14\. Apparitions

Determine: \- Exact location \- What the location looked like at that
date \- Number and ages of visionaries \- Their clothing \- Who else was
present \- What witnesses could or could not see \- Historical description
of the apparition \- Weather, landscape, and time of day

Do not depict churches, shrines, statues, basilicas, or monuments
constructed after the apparition\.

## 15\. Martyrdom

Treat martyrdom with dignity\. Emphasize courage, witness, prayer, human
emotion, and historical circumstances\. Avoid unnecessary gore or
sensational violence\.

## 16\. Jesus, Mary, and Biblical Figures

Use historically plausible Middle Eastern appearance for first\-century
Jewish figures\.

Avoid modern Europeanized or Americanized appearances unless justified
by a specific artistic context\. Clothing, buildings, landscapes, tools,
and customs should reflect biblical geography and period\.

## 17\. Known Historical Appearance

When reliable photographs, contemporary portraits, death masks, or
descriptions exist, use them as references for facial structure, hair,
age, body type, and clothing\.

Do not unnecessarily beautify or modernize the person\.

## 18\. Human Diversity and Individual Appearance

Do not default to conventionally attractive, heroic, highly masculine,
muscular, or idealized people\.

The collection should look like a genuine cross\-section of humanity\.

### Avoid the Default “Hero” Appearance

Unless supported by historical evidence, do not automatically give male
subjects: \- Broad muscular shoulders \- Large athletic builds \- Strong
square jaws \- Thick necks \- Perfect facial symmetry \- Full heads of
thick hair \- Perfectly groomed hair \- Heavy masculine facial features \-
Modern American\-looking physiques \- Fashion\-model or movie\-star
attractiveness

Likewise, do not automatically beautify women or give them contemporary
fashion\-model features\.

### Deliberately Vary Physical Appearance

When appearance is unknown, intentionally vary: \- Thin, average, stocky,
slight, lanky, or heavy builds \- Narrow, rounded, or sloping shoulders \-
Long, round, narrow, broad, or irregular faces \- High, low, broad,
narrow, or unusually shaped foreheads \- Receding or uneven hairlines \-
Balding crowns and male\-pattern baldness \- Thin, thick, curly, straight,
coarse, fine, or wavy hair \- Large, small, crooked, broad, narrow, or
prominent noses \- Large or small ears \- Heavy or delicate eyebrows \-
Deep\-set or prominent eyes \- Strong, soft, recessed, or narrow chins \-
Wrinkles and lines around the eyes \- Weathered skin and hands \- Facial
asymmetry \- Different beard densities and growth patterns

These characteristics should remain believable and never become
caricatures\.

### Age Must Be Visible

Do not make everyone appear 30–40 years old\.

Allow realistic signs of age: receding and graying hair, thinning hair,
wrinkles, softer jawlines, changing body composition, stooped posture
when appropriate, weathered hands, and tired eyes\.

### Body Type Should Follow the Person’s Life

A scholar should not automatically have the physique of a modern
athlete\. A fasting monk may be thin\. A laborer may have a strong working
build without looking like a bodybuilder\. An elderly bishop may have
narrow shoulders or a soft or heavy build\. A young person may be slight
or awkward\.

Avoid modern gym\-developed physiques unless specifically justified\.

### Preserve Known Imperfections

When historical evidence exists, preserve distinctive characteristics
such as baldness, unusual hair, prominent ears or noses, facial
asymmetry, unusual height, heavy or slight build, wrinkles, and
distinctive facial structure\.

Do not replace them with a more attractive generic face\.

### Geography and Ancestry

Physical appearance should be plausible for the person’s actual
geographic and ancestral background\.

Do not default historical Europeans to contemporary American\-looking
faces, and do not make people from different regions appear
interchangeable\.

Avoid stereotypes while seeking believable human variation within each
population\.

### Faces Should Not Repeat

Avoid repeatedly generating the same jawline, nose, hairstyle, beard,
body type, facial proportions, age, or expression\.

The collection should look like hundreds of genuinely different human
beings, not the same small group of AI\-generated actors wearing
different historical costumes\.

### Do Not Confuse Holiness With Physical Beauty

A saint may be plain, awkward, balding, heavy, thin, short, weathered,
elderly, physically unimpressive, or unusual\-looking\.

Holiness should emerge from what the person is doing, how they relate to
others, and the story being depicted—not idealized physical
perfection\.

## 19\. Avoid Repetition Across the Collection

Avoid producing hundreds of variations of “saint sitting at wooden desk
beside candle\.”

Vary: \- Indoor/outdoor setting \- Camera angle \- Activity \- Number of
people \- Time of day \- Environment \- Composition \- Distance from subject

The collection should feel like a photographic journey through Catholic
history\.

## 20\. Quality Review

Before accepting an image, check:

### Historical

- Correct period, clothing, architecture, geography
- No obvious anachronisms

### Human

- Correct number and approximate ages of people
- Natural hands, eyes, proportions, and interactions
- Distinctive rather than repetitive faces and body types

### Visual

- Bright enough
- Faces clearly visible
- No excessive orange/brown cast
- Natural color and good contrast
- Important action clearly visible
- Works on a phone\-sized display

### Story

Ask: **Does this image actually communicate something about this**
**person’s life or the event?**

If not, regenerate\.

## 21\. Brightness Rejection Rule

Reject or regenerate when: \- Important faces disappear into shadow \- The
overall image is unnecessarily dark \- Brown/orange tones dominate \-
Whites appear strongly yellow or brown \- Details will be difficult to
see on a phone \- Candlelight unnecessarily becomes the primary light
source \- The image requires significant manual brightening afterward

A finished image should normally be usable without post\-processing to
increase brightness\.

## 22\. Final Prompt Construction

Before generation, construct a visual specification:

**SUBJECT** — Who is depicted and approximate age\.

**ACTION** — What is actually happening\.

**DATE** — Year or historical period\.

**LOCATION** — Specific geographic and physical setting\.

**CLOTHING** — Historically appropriate clothing\.

**APPEARANCE** — Historically known characteristics or, if unknown, a
deliberately selected realistic and non\-idealized human appearance\.

**ENVIRONMENT** — Architecture, landscape, furnishings, tools, and
other people\.

**LIGHTING** — Bright, realistic natural illumination appropriate to
the scene\.

**COMPOSITION** — Candid historical photograph, portrait 2:3,
environmental context visible\.

**HISTORICAL RESTRICTIONS** — Important things that must not appear\.

**STYLE** — Photorealistic, natural skin and textures, historically
believable, documentary\-like rather than staged\.

## 23\. Guiding Principles

**If a photographer had actually been present at this moment in Catholic**
**history, what might the photograph have looked like?**

Create that image\.

For a person whose appearance is unknown, also ask:

**What would an ordinary real person of this age, place, period,**
**vocation, and circumstances plausibly have looked like?**

Generate that person rather than a modern heroic ideal\.
